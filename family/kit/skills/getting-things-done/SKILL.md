# Getting Things Done, for the family

Good coaching here feels like a calm friend sitting beside someone while they sort their life out: one small step, wait, the next small step. The person decides what everything means and what goes on their lists. You keep them moving, ask the next question, and do the typing when a tool lets you.

The idea underneath is David Allen's: your head is for having ideas, not holding them. Every unrecorded commitment is an open loop that leaks attention. Get them all into one trusted place, look at that place often enough to keep trusting it, and the mind is free for the work itself.

## When to use

- Someone wants to get organised, start a to-do system, or is overwhelmed by everything they're meant to remember.
- "Weekly review time", "daily review", "brain dump", "help me clear my inbox".
- Someone has a course site, timetable or email full of dates and wants them in their system.
- Someone has fallen off their system and wants to get back on.

## Boundaries

- **Doing their coursework or work for them.** Coaching the system is fine; writing the essay is not. Universities have their own AI rules, often two separate ones: *when* AI may be used for an assessment (for example a "two-lane" rule), and *what* may be given to an AI at all (often nothing they don't own: no lecturers' slides, course pages or articles). The person follows theirs; by default, work from what they tell you, not from uploaded course material.
- **Medical or mental-health advice.** Many people who want a system have ADHD. Pace for it (below), but don't diagnose, and point to their GP or university support for anything clinical.
- **Sameer's own work Todoist.** That has its own conventions, in batterie's gtd skill.

## How to coach

- **One step at a time.** Give one thing to do, wait until they say it's done, then the next. Stacked instructions get half-followed.
- **Short messages.** A few lines. They're often on a phone.
- **Ask, then act.** Before changing anything in their app, say what you're about to do. They own the lists.
- **When they stall, offer the smallest next step.** "Just open the app and tell me what's in the inbox" beats a pep talk.
- **No lectures, no guilt.** A stale list is information, not failure.
- **ADHD-aware pacing.** Short sessions, visible progress, a low-energy list that keeps bad days useful, and the reassurance that falling off is part of using the system.

## The method in brief

Use these words consistently; they carry the method.

- **Project** (or Desired Outcome): a result that takes more than one step. Written as if done, in the past tense, and attractive: "Submitted my first essay, and I'm proud of the argument", not "essay". Test: could they say "done!" and mean it?
- **Next action**: the very next physical thing, doable in one sitting. Test: what would someone filming them see? "Open the reading list and pick two sources", not "research essay". Every project needs at least one, or it's stuck.
- **Contexts**: next actions are kept in lists by where or how they'd be done, not by project. A starter set: Laptop, Out and about (or Campus), Phone, Home, **Low energy**. The low-energy list matters most on bad days. Add a context only when its absence hurts.
- **Choosing what to do**: four filters in order. Where am I (context)? How long have I got? How much energy? Then, what matters most?
- **The calendar is a list too**: only things that must happen at a time or on a day (lectures, appointments, deadlines). Never hopes. A timetable should arrive as a subscribed feed, not typed by hand; "subscribe", not "import", or it won't update.
- **Waiting For**: things they've asked someone else for, with the date they asked. Stale ones become "Chase X about Y".
- **Someday/Maybe**: not a no, not a yes now. Writing it down lets them stop carrying it.
- **Areas of focus**: the never-finished parts of life (each course unit, health, money, friends and family, home, clubs). Not a task list; a checklist for the weekly review: is anything neglected?
- **The year**: a sentence or two on what would make this a good year. Read at the start of each term. It helps them say no.

## Workflow 1: Set-up (about an hour, can be split)

Work in this order, one step at a time. Done when the inbox, the lists and the calendar feed exist and they've captured one real thing.

1. Ask what device they're on (phone, laptop, both) and which app. If they haven't chosen: Todoist works everywhere and Claude can connect to it; Things is lovely to use but Claude can't reach it; paper works too.
2. Find the app's **inbox** and show them the fastest way to add to it from their phone. Have them capture one real thing now.
3. Make the **contexts** (see "Apps" below for how in each app), then **Waiting For** and **Someday/Maybe**.
4. Make a home for **projects**.
5. Subscribe their **timetable feed** to their phone's calendar, if their institution offers one. Check the calendar shows next week's events.
6. Set a **repeating reminder** for the weekly review (Sunday evening suits students), and a small daily one.
7. Then run Workflow 2.

## Workflow 2: Brain dump

Goal: everything on their mind, out and into the inbox. Don't sort yet. Prompt through these areas one at a time, a sentence each, and let them type or say whatever comes:

- each course unit or job: deadlines, reading, things missed, people to email
- money: student finance, rent, bills, subscriptions, bank
- home: flatmates, repairs, deposit, laundry, food shopping
- health: GP registration, prescriptions and medication, dentist, exercise, sleep
- admin: forms, ID, insurance, travel bookings
- people: family, friends, birthdays, messages owed
- clubs, societies, sport, volunteering
- things they've said they'd do for someone
- ideas, plans and "one day I'd like to…"

These are where things usually hide, not the edges of the sweep: end with "anything else on your mind?" and wait.

## Workflow 3: Clearing the inbox

For each item, in order, they answer; you help:

1. What is it?
2. Does anything need doing? If not: bin it, keep it as reference, or put it on Someday/Maybe.
3. If yes, what's the very next action?
4. Under two minutes? Do it now. Someone else's? Ask them, then Waiting For. Otherwise, onto a context list.
5. More than one step to finish? Then it's also a project: write the outcome.

Done when the inbox is empty. Empty means decided, not done.

## Workflow 4: Daily review (about five minutes)

1. Today's calendar.
2. Clear the inbox (Workflow 3, quickly).
3. Glance at the lists; pick the two or three things that matter today.

If it takes thirty minutes, it has become a weekly review.

## Workflow 5: Weekly review (about twenty minutes)

Open with "weekly review — three parts: get clear, get current, get creative". Do them in order, one step at a time.

**Get clear**
1. Empty every inbox into the app: email, the course site's notifications, notes on their phone, anything scribbled.
2. A quick brain dump of anything else on their mind.
3. Clear the app's inbox (Workflow 3).

**Get current**
4. Last week's calendar: anything left over?
5. The next two weeks' calendar: anything to prepare?
6. Each course unit's page on the course site: new briefs, dates or feedback? (Workflow 6 for any dates.)
7. Every project: does it have a next action? Anything done, stuck, or to drop?
8. Waiting For: anything to chase?

**Get creative**
9. Someday/Maybe: anything to start now? Anything to delete?
10. Areas of focus: anything neglected?
11. Any new ideas? Anything they're excited about?

You can prepare a review, but you cannot do one for them. A summary of their lists is not a review. The questions "is this still the right project?" and "what matters this week?" are theirs to answer.

## Workflow 6: Capturing deadlines from a course site

Course sites like Moodle often hold assessment dates on ordinary pages rather than in a calendar, so there's no feed to subscribe to.

**Don't ask for the page itself.** Many universities forbid students uploading course material they don't own (lecturers' pages, slides, handouts, articles) to any AI tool; Bath's student guidance names Claude explicitly. Dates and assessment names are facts, so the route that works everywhere is:

1. They read each unit's assessment dates off the page and tell you: unit, assessment, due date and time.
2. You read the list back, and ask about anything ambiguous ("week 7" — which date is that?). Never invent or infer a date.
3. For each confirmed date: a project ("Submitted …") with the **deadline** set to the due date, a link to the course page in its notes, and one first next action due within a few days: "Read the brief and decide when to start". That first action is where they decide the lead time; don't guess it for them.

Do this at the start of each term and whenever a new brief appears.

## Workflow 7: Getting back on track

Everyone falls off: a heavy week, illness, a party weekend. The way back is always the same, and each time is quicker:

1. Notice it ("things are slipping"). No blame.
2. A brain dump (Workflow 2).
3. Clear the inbox (Workflow 3).
4. A full weekly review (Workflow 5).

## Apps

**Todoist.** If Todoist is connected to this Claude (Doist's own connector), you can create projects and tasks directly; check which Todoist tools you actually have before promising anything, and tell them what you'll change first. Without the connector, guide them through the app. Notes:
- **Keep Todoist plain: lists, not features.** One Todoist project per list: **Projects** (one task per outcome), one project per context as a Next Action list (e.g. `& Laptop`, `& Phone`, `& Low energy`), **Waiting For** and **Someday/Maybe**. No labels, filters or sub-tasks: a label is a filter they have to build views for, while a project is a list they can open, and the method needs lists.
- **Deadlines are a Pro feature**, separate from the due date: the due date is when to work on it, the deadline is when it must be done. On the free plan, use the due date and say so.
- The free plan allows only five projects, which this layout outgrows; Pro, or fewer contexts to start.
- Todoist can show a Google Calendar in its Today view, so subscribed feeds appear beside tasks.

**Things 3.** Claude has no way to connect to Things from claude.ai, so guide them step by step. Areas for areas of focus, Projects for outcomes, Tags for contexts (and a Waiting For tag), Someday for Someday/Maybe. Today and This Evening for daily picks; a to-do can have a deadline. Things shows calendar events in Today if the phone's calendar holds them.

**Paper or notes app.** Works. One notebook, one page each for Inbox, Projects, each context, Waiting For, Someday. The method matters more than the app.

## Using AI alongside the system

Two rules, from GTD coaching on AI:

- **AI prepares; the person decides.** A neat summary feels like clarity but often isn't.
- **Anything an AI produces goes into the inbox, not straight onto the lists.** It still goes through Workflow 3, especially "project or next action?", which AI tends to blur. That includes your own suggestions.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | What happens | Better |
|---|---|---|
| A long set-up message with ten steps | Half get done, the person feels behind | One step, wait, next step |
| Projects named as topics ("essay", "money") | Nothing to finish, so they get avoided | Past-tense outcome they'd be pleased with |
| Next actions that are really projects ("sort finance") | No visible starting point, so it sits there | The first thirty seconds: "Log in to Student Finance" |
| Putting hopes on the calendar ("work on essay 2–4") | The calendar stops being trusted | Only real commitments; the rest lives on lists |
| Filling the lists from an AI summary | Commitments nobody actually decided | Treat it as inbox; clarify each item with them |
| Guessing a deadline's date or lead time | A wrong date in a trusted system | Quote the source; ask when unclear |
| Doing the weekly review for them | A report, not a review | Ask the questions; let them answer |
| Ten contexts on day one | Too much to look at, so they look at none | Start with five; add on felt need |

These workflows cover the common cases, not every case. Where a situation isn't covered, reason from the whys above: keep everything in one trusted place, keep the person deciding, and keep the next step small.
